## About

My first construction paycheck came in 1984. I was a geotechnical technician at Hardin-Kight Associates, working college breaks, running sand-cone density tests on subgrade in whatever weather showed up that day. I spent the thirty-five years after that in heavy civil. Estimating first, then project management, and eventually the seat where somebody has to answer for the numbers. In all that time I can count on one hand the jobs that went bad because a person couldn't read a set of plans. What actually cost us money was almost always a conversation that never happened, or one that happened badly. That's the problem I work on now. I train contractors on two things. The first is how to build the number. Estimating, cost control, project management, and supervision, including the AGC of America curriculum (PMDP, STP, CSF) that I deliver for contractors and chapters around the country. The second is how to lead the people who have to hit that number. For that I use a framework I call the Three Layers, which looks at how a person is wired, how that wiring sounds to everybody else, and what actually motivates them underneath all of it. It runs on the Big Five, GiANT's 5 Voices, and the Valuegraphics Values Identifier. I'm certified in the last two. Most companies buy one of those halves and then wonder why the other one keeps costing them. I teach both, usually in the same room, in language the field and the office can both use. There's decent data behind this. Contractors who build structured training programs see rework go down, workers' comp go down, and retention go up. One company in the NCCER research took retention from 28% to 73% over three years, with the same people they already had. I write most weeks in a newsletter called Culture Under Construction, and I'm working on a book, Middle of the Arena. If you run a contracting company, lead people inside one, or serve them through an association, I'd like to hear what's giving you trouble. … more
